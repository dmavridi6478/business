# The ten frameworks in detail

Source: ten one-page graphics by Natan Mohart (Batch 117), each summarising a McKinsey framework. The graphics are a third party's summaries: the statistics and "real cases" they quote were not verified against McKinsey's publications here. The worked numbers were recomputed (see "Checked" lines).

## 1. Influence Model (change)
First introduced in McKinsey Quarterly, "The Psychology of Change Management" (2003). Card claim: successful transformations were nearly 8 times more likely to use all four levers than just one (McKinsey Global Survey, 2015); treat the multiplier as the card's claim.

| Lever | Employees should say | Why it works | Ask your team | Red flag | Your move |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Understanding and conviction | "I understand what is being asked of me, and it makes sense." | People want actions to match beliefs | "Could a frontline employee explain why we're changing, in their own words?" | Leaders assume the "why" is obvious and skip it | Write a change story (where we're going, why, why it matters to me), then listen to how it lands |
| 2 Formal mechanisms | "I see structures, processes and incentives that support the change." | Behaviour follows what is rewarded and measured | "Which KPI or bonus quietly rewards the old behaviour?" | A refiner wanted reliable maintenance but rewarded cutting maintenance spend | Audit KPIs and bonuses against the new behaviour; money alone won't do it |
| 3 Talent and skills | "I have the skills and the chance to work in the new way." | Adult brains keep learning; confidence drives effort | "Who needs a new skill on day 1, and where will they practise it?" | A big launch with a one-off training day and nothing after | Train in the flow of work, give early wins, show examples of good |
| 4 Role modelling | "I see leaders and colleagues behaving differently." | People copy those around them, often without noticing | "Who do people actually listen to, regardless of title?" | "Empowerment" from the stage, sign-off needed for every decision | Find the informal opinion leaders, not just the C-suite |

Run a 4-lever check: (1) write the four "employees should say" statements for your change; (2) survey a sample of affected people and score each 1-5; (3) list planned actions under each lever (most plans have one or two empty); (4) fill the weakest lever first, then repeat the survey in 90 days. Pros' take: communication alone is lever 1 of 4; your KPIs are your real change story; pick role models by influence, not title.

## 2. Consumer Decision Journey (marketing)
Core idea: the funnel assumes buyers narrow choices; McKinsey's research found shoppers add brands while they evaluate and loyal customers skip the funnel. Card stats: about 20,000 consumers across 5 industries and 3 continents; 3x more likely to be bought if you are in the initial shortlist; two thirds of touch points in evaluation are consumer-driven; 6x gap in active vs passive loyalists between major car insurers.

| Stage | What happens | Your move | Track |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Initial consideration | A trigger hits; a first shortlist forms from memory and impressions | Build salience before the need appears | Share of consideration set |
| 2 Active evaluation | Brands are added and dropped; the funnel widens | Win reviews, comparisons and word of mouth | Share of search, review rating |
| 3 Moment of purchase | Final call at shelf, checkout or pricing page | Own packaging, placement and checkout | Conversion at point of sale |
| 4 Postpurchase | The customer uses the product and keeps researching | Onboard, educate, make them look smart | Repeat rate, NPS |
| Loyalty loop | Happy customers skip consideration; unhappy ones reopen the search | Turn passive loyalists into active ones | Active loyalist share, referrals |

Audit in 4 steps: survey recent buyers (where did they first consider you, who did they add later); find the stage where you lose the most people vs your top rival; move budget there even if it means less top-of-funnel spend; count active vs passive loyalists.

## 3. Three Horizons of Growth (portfolio over time)
| | H1 Extend and defend the core | H2 Build emerging businesses | H3 Create options for the future |
|---|---|---|---|
| What | Core businesses that generate most of today's profit and cash | Rising ventures with real customers and revenue | Research, pilots, minority stakes |
| Ask | Where are we losing share or margin we could defend? | Which bet has real traction and needs capital to scale? | What shift could make our core irrelevant in 5 years? |
| Measure by | Profit, cash flow, margin, ROIC | Revenue growth, share, unit economics | Assumptions validated, milestones hit |
| Fund like | Annual budget, efficiency targets | Staged capital tied to milestones | Many small bets, venture style |
| Led by | Operators who optimise | Builders who can scale | Explorers at ease with ambiguity |
| Kill rule | Declining returns on reinvestment | Unit economics don't improve with scale | Key assumption proven wrong |
| Common trap | Starving H3 to hit this quarter's H1 numbers | Judging them on H1 margins before they can scale | Endless pilots with no path to H2 |
| Card example (Amazon, mid-2000s) | Online retail | Marketplace and Prime (2005) | AWS (2006) |

Run in one session: list every initiative and tag H1, H2 or H3; compare budget and leadership hours per horizon (many find H3 close to zero); give each horizon its own scorecard (one set of KPIs kills H2 and H3); set graduation criteria. Misreadings: it is not a sequence (all three run concurrently); fixed time frames aren't in the original; Steve Blank's critique is that disruption can now arrive as fast as H1 work.

## 4. Business System Framework (find your edge)
Created at McKinsey in 1980 (Bales, Chatterjee, Gogel and Puri), per the card five years before Porter's Value Chain. Map each step from technology to after-sales service; advantage comes from choices rivals don't make and from steps that reinforce one value proposition.

| Step | Key choices | Ask | Benchmark | Reinvented by (card) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Technology | Build, buy or license; which bets | Which tech must we own, which can we rent? | R&D as % of revenue, time to market | ARM licenses chip designs instead of making chips |
| 2 Product design | Features, cost target, modularity | What could we remove that customers wouldn't miss? | Design cost per unit, parts count | IKEA designs furniture to ship flat |
| 3 Manufacturing | Make or buy, scale, location | Which steps would a partner do cheaper and better? | Unit cost, capacity utilisation | Apple designs in house, outsources assembly |
| 4 Marketing | Segment, price, brand, message | Where do customers pay attention that rivals ignore? | CAC, share of voice, price premium | Red Bull builds its brand on extreme sports |
| 5 Distribution | Channels, logistics, inventory | Which middleman takes margin without adding value? | Cost to serve, days of inventory | Dell sells PCs direct, built to order |
| 6 Service | Support, warranty, repairs, pricing | Could we sell an outcome instead of a product? | Retention, service margin | Rolls-Royce sells engine "power by the hour" |

At every step ask: how well do we perform here, is there a better way, how do competitors do it, who has the lower cost here. Run it: map your six steps next to your top rival's with cost per step; circle the step where you could make a choice no one else makes; test fit (does it strengthen every other step?); before an acquisition overlay the target's system on yours. Business System finds the move; Porter's Value Chain (adds HR, procurement, infrastructure, margin) costs it.

## 5. McKinsey 7S Model (alignment)
Already in `mckinsey-7s-model`. The graphic adds, per S, a question and a red flag:

| S | Ask your team | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy | What will we do differently from rivals in the next 3 years? | Strategy changed, structure and incentives didn't |
| Structure | Who decides, and how many layers sit between the top and the customer? | Every decision escalates to the top; reorgs every year |
| Systems | What do our bonuses and dashboards actually reward? | Strategy says innovation, KPIs reward short-term output |
| Shared values | What really gets people promoted here? | Values on the wall that no one uses to make a decision |
| Style | Where does the leadership team's calendar really go? | "Empowerment" in the deck, sign-off needed for every step |
| Staff | Are the right people in the 10 roles that matter most? | Critical roles filled by whoever is available |
| Skills | Which capability does the strategy need that we lack today? | A new digital strategy with no one in-house who can build it |

Run a 7S diagnosis in 4 steps: score today (1-5 per element with people from different levels); define the target; check the 21 links between elements (green, amber, red); fix the red links, starting where the gap is widest.

## 6. Portfolio of Initiatives (strategy under uncertainty)
Lowell Bryan, McKinsey, early 2000s, building on Three Horizons and Courtney's levels of uncertainty. Strategy as natural selection: search, monitor, reinvest, adjust. Map every initiative by horizon (when it pays off) and by how well you know the game (distinctive, shared, new).

| Type | What | Use when | Where on the map |
|---|---|---|---|
| No-regrets moves | Pay off whatever the future brings (cut costs, gather competitive intelligence, build skills) | Always: they fund everything else | Mostly Horizon 1, familiar ground |
| Options | Small bets that keep the upside open and cap the downside (pilots, limited JVs, licensing) | You can't yet tell which future wins | Horizons 2 and 3, wherever knowledge is new |
| Big bets | Large commitments that win big in some futures, lose big in others | You know more than rivals, or uncertainty has narrowed | Only in the Distinctive row, never on unfamiliar ground |

Name your level of uncertainty first: 1 clear enough future (one forecast is good enough), 2 alternative futures (a few discrete scenarios), 3 range of futures (for example 10 to 30% penetration), 4 true ambiguity. Rule of thumb: the higher the level, the more the portfolio should lean on options and no-regrets moves, with fewer big bets. Build in 4 steps: list every initiative with budget, owner and expected payoff date; plot by horizon and familiarity and tag bet, option or no-regrets; check balance (big bets only where knowledge is distinctive); set review gates (what result earns more capital, what result kills it). Failure modes: everything piles into Horizon 1; big bets on unfamiliar ground; options with no kill criteria.

## 7. Strategic Control Map (hunter or hunted)
Developed at McKinsey in 1996 by Vijay D'Silva, Bob Fallon and Asheet Mehta. Plot each company in an industry by size (book value, log scale) and performance (market to book). Math: market cap = book value x market to book. Same market cap, two routes: a $20B balance sheet at 1.5x, or a $6B one at 5x (Checked: both equal $30B; the second is far harder to take over cheaply).

| Zone | Meaning | Risk | Move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic control (large, highly valued) | Least likely to be acquired | Diluting returns while chasing growth | Hunt: buy undervalued assets, enter adjacent markets |
| Niche performers (small, highly valued) | Often on IP, skills or brand | One bad year makes you a bargain | Protect performance, scale before a giant buys you |
| Underperforming giants (big balance sheet, weak returns) | Targets for cost consolidation by stronger rivals | Being consolidated | Fix returns fast, sell what drags the multiple |
| Takeover targets (small, weakly valued) | Most exposed | Losing control of your own future | Fix the core, divest losers, or pick your buyer |

Build in 4 steps: pull book value and market cap for 10 to 20 peers in one industry; plot on log scales split at peer medians; plot 5 years of history; decide who you could buy, who could buy you and what move keeps you in control. Where it misleads: compare within one industry only; asset-light firms look small on book value; sentiment swings. Questions: "If our multiple fell 30% tomorrow, who could afford to buy us?" "Which peer below the median would make us bigger without diluting returns?" "Where have we moved on this map over the last 5 years, and why?"

## 8. Industry Cost Curve (prices, price wars, who survives)
Eight producers sorted from cheapest to most expensive; bar width = capacity, bar height = cash cost per unit; price is set by the last producer needed to meet demand; your margin is price minus your bar. Card example: demand 70M units sets price $50 (producer G, the marginal one); producer H at $61 is out of the money. If demand drops 21% to 55M, price falls to $36, a 28% drop (Checked: 0.79 x 70M = 55.3M and (50-36)/50 = 28%).

Five strategy questions it answers: where will price settle if demand falls; who exits first in a downturn (producers on the far right); should we add capacity (new cheap capacity pushes the curve right and lowers price for everyone, including you); which assets are worth buying (left-side assets keep earning through the cycle); how much cost do we need to cut (enough to move left of the price line in your worst demand scenario). Build in 4 steps: list producers and capacity; estimate cash cost per unit with one consistent definition; sort cheapest to most expensive and stack the bars; overlay 2 or 3 demand scenarios and read the price off each. Where it breaks: built for commodities; messy when producers serve several segments; input prices, currency and technology reshuffle the curve.

## 9. GE-McKinsey Matrix (where to invest, hold or exit)
Built by McKinsey for General Electric in the early 1970s as a richer alternative to the BCG matrix. Y axis industry attractiveness ("Is this a good market to be in?": market size and growth, margins, competitive intensity, cyclicality, regulation, disruption risk). X axis business unit strength ("Can we win in it?": relative share, cost position, brand, differentiation, channels, IP, capabilities). Zones: green = invest or grow (capital, talent, M&A firepower); yellow = selectivity (invest only in segments where you can win); red = harvest or divest (minimise capital, maximise cash, plan the exit).

Score in 5 steps: pick 4 to 6 factors per axis; agree weights summing to 100% before anyone scores; score each factor 1 to 5, backed by data; multiply and add (below 2.33 Low, 2.33 to 3.67 Medium, above 3.67 High); plot each unit with bubbles sized by revenue and draw where it is heading.

Worked example, Unit A. Industry attractiveness: market growth 30% x 4 = 1.20; profit margins 25% x 3 = 0.75; competitive intensity 20% x 2 = 0.40; market size 15% x 5 = 0.75; regulatory risk 10% x 3 = 0.30; total 3.40. Business unit strength: relative market share 30% x 4 = 1.20; cost position 25% x 2 = 0.50; capabilities 25% x 3 = 0.75; brand strength 20% x 3 = 0.60; total 3.05. (Checked: both totals recomputed.) Both are Medium, so Unit A lands in Selectivity: fix the weak cost position before adding capital. BCG uses two single factors (fast, coarse); GE-McKinsey uses weighted bundles on both axes (more nuance, more data). Pitfalls: subjective weights; a static snapshot (rescore yearly); units in isolation (a "Divest" unit may feed customers or tech to a "Grow" one).

## 10. SCP Framework (why some industries print money)
Structure, Conduct, Performance. Origin: 1930s Edward Mason (Harvard); 1950s Joe Bain, used for antitrust; 1980 Porter brings it to strategy (Five Forces); 1980s McKinsey adds feedback loops (your moves can reshape the industry), so you can play scenarios forward before you act. External shocks (technology, regulation, demand, input costs) hit all three.

| | Structure: how attractive is the playing field? | Conduct: how do players compete? | Performance: who wins, and how much? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Measure | Concentration (top 4 share, HHI); barriers to entry and exit; demand growth and cyclicality; product differentiation; cost structure (fixed vs variable); buyer and supplier power | Pricing (discipline or price wars); capacity additions and exits; R&D and innovation spend; advertising and branding; M&A and alliances; lobbying and legal moves | ROIC vs cost of capital; margins and their spread; revenue and share growth; productivity and innovation; customer value and prices; exits and bankruptcies |
| Ask | Which structural change in the next 3 years would hurt us most? | What will rivals do if we cut price or add capacity? | Is our profit from market position or from being better? |

Card example, US airlines: shock (1978 Airline Deregulation Act ends federal control of fares and routes) → structure (low-cost entrants flood in, barriers fall) → conduct (fare wars and capacity races) → performance (fares fall, waves of losses and bankruptcies) → feedback (merger wave 2008 to 2013; the card says the largest carriers now fly about two thirds of US domestic traffic). Run in 4 steps: map today's structure with numbers, not adjectives; list the 3 shocks most likely to hit in the next 5 years; for each, play forward how rivals behave and what happens to margins; pick the move that shapes structure in your favour, not just the one that wins this year. Challenged by: the Chicago School (high profits may come from efficiency, not market power); messy causality; game theory has largely replaced it in economics, though in strategy work it is still a sharp first lens.
