# 7 principles of powerful storytelling

Source: an infographic by Natan Mohart (Batch 119). The quote at the centre, "The most powerful person in the world is the storyteller", is attributed on the card to Steve Jobs; I did not verify a primary source, so cite it as "attributed to".

| # | Principle | Rule on the card | Why it works (card's claim) | Example line from the card | Check (added) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Start with tension | Not emotion or facts; start with inner or outer conflict | The brain engages when it senses a gap between "what is" and "what should be" | "Everything looked successful on paper. But inside, I knew something was breaking." | Does line one contain a gap, a conflict or a risk? |
| 2 | Make it human | Show doubt, fear or a mistake, not a polished version of yourself | People trust real experience, not performance | "I didn't have the answer. And for the first time, I admitted it to my team." | Is there one real mistake or doubt, true and specific? |
| 3 | Speak in images | Do not explain the idea; show a scene the audience can visualise | Images are processed faster than words and remembered longer | "The room went quiet. Everyone avoided eye contact. That's when I knew the decision was wrong." | Can the reader picture a place, a person and an action? |
| 4 | Make it about them | The story reflects the listener's inner world, not your ego | People engage when they recognise themselves | "If you've ever felt stuck in a role that looks great from the outside, this will sound familiar." | Would the target reader say "that is me" by paragraph two? |
| 5 | Show the stakes | Show what can be lost, not just what can be gained | Loss aversion is more powerful than promised rewards | "Doing nothing felt safe. Until I realized it was the most expensive choice." | Is the cost of inaction concrete (time, money, trust)? |
| 6 | Close the loop | Return to the beginning and show what changed | Closed loops increase memorability and create completion | "That fear I mentioned at the start never disappeared. But it stopped controlling me." | Does the ending answer the opening tension? |
| 7 | Leave a decision | A good story ends with a choice, not a lesson | Stories shape behaviour when they invite action | "So the real question isn't what I did. It's what you'll do next time." | Is there one decision or next step for the reader? |

## Rules (added)
- **True stories only.** Do not invent a scene, a quote or a customer to satisfy principle 3 or 2. Change names and details with permission, and say so if a story is composite.
- **Stakes are not fear-mongering.** Principle 5 should state a real cost; do not manufacture urgency or use loss framing to push a purchase (see `marketing-psychology:dark-pattern-check`).
- **The psychology claims are the card's.** Loss aversion is a documented finding; "images are processed faster" and "closed loops increase memorability" are common claims I did not verify here.
- **One story, one point.** Seven principles do not mean seven beats in 300 words; use as many as serve the point.

## Method
1. Write the point in one sentence, and the decision you want the reader to make.
2. Find the tension: what was the gap between what was and what should have been?
3. Draft the scene (people, place, line of dialogue), then the mistake, then the cost, then the change.
4. Return to the first line and close the loop.
5. Audit against the seven checks above; fix the weakest first.

Run with `/story-check <draft>` or `/tell-story <point and raw facts>`.
