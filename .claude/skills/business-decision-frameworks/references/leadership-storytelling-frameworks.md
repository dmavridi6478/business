# Leadership Storytelling Frameworks

Source: reviewed from an uploaded infographic — "6 Storytelling
Frameworks That Help You Lead Inspiringly" (Kelly Knight). Six named
narrative structures for leading through change, vision, and setbacks —
distinct from `storytelling-pyramid.md` in this same reference set: the
Minto Pyramid structures a persuasive *argument* (answer-first, then
evidence) for a slide or exec summary; these six structure a *story*
(narrative arc) for a vision, a change, or a shared moment. Use the
pyramid to make a recommendation land; use one of these six to make a
message memorable and rally people around it.

## 1. Pixar's Story Spine (Kenn Adams, Pixar)

A fill-in-the-blanks sentence structure mirroring a classic narrative
arc: **Once upon a time... Every day... But one day... Because of
that... Until finally...** Useful for shaping any message (a vision, a
change, a setback) into a story people remember instead of a bullet list
they forget.

## 2. The Golden Circle (Simon Sinek)

Communicate from the inside out: start with **Why**, then **How**, then
**What** — the reverse of how most announcements are actually written
(What first). Useful for leading from purpose, not task — people commit
to the why long before the what.

## 3. Duarte's Sparkline (Nancy Duarte)

Move people by repeatedly contrasting **what is** with **what could
be** — today's reality against the future being called toward, as an
oscillating line rather than a single before/after. Useful for rallying a
team around change, making the gap between today and what's possible
impossible to ignore.

## 4. The Hero's Journey (Joseph Campbell)

The path from an ordinary world, through a challenge, to
transformation and return. Useful for framing a team member as the hero
and the leader as the guide — the story that builds people up, not one
that centers the leader.

## 5. And, But, Therefore (Randy Olson)

A three-word narrative spine: **setup and... complication but...
resolution therefore...** Example: *"We were growing and the team was
proud, but we'd outgrown how we worked, therefore we changed how we
run."* Useful for turning a rambling update into one clear, compelling
line — likely the fastest of the six to make any message resonate.

## 6. What, So What, Now What?

A three-step structure that turns any event into a shared lesson: **what
happened** (the facts), **why it matters** (the interpretation), **what
we do next** (the action). Useful for team debriefs, quarterly reviews,
or after a setback — turning experience into direction everyone leaves
aligned on.

## How to use these

1. Match the framework to the moment, not the other way around — a
   quarterly debrief wants What/So What/Now What or And-But-Therefore
   (compression), a change-management push wants the Sparkline or Golden
   Circle (contrast/purpose), a recognition moment wants the Hero's
   Journey (centers someone else).
2. These are narrative tools for *spoken or written leadership moments*
   (a town hall, a change announcement, a team debrief) — not a
   replacement for `storytelling-pyramid.md` when the deliverable is a
   data-backed recommendation that needs to survive a skeptical reader's
   scrutiny.
3. Same caveat as this skill's other reference files: infographic-sourced
   summaries, accurate but shallow — read Pixar's actual Story Spine
   writeup, *Start with Why*, or *Resonate* (Duarte) before using one of
   these for a genuinely high-stakes leadership moment.
