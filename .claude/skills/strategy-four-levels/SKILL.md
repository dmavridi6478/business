# The four levels of strategy

Source: an infographic by Igor Buinevici (WildCapital), "4 Levels of Strategy", closing with a quotation attributed to Henry Mintzberg: "Strategy is a pattern in a stream of decisions." (Batch 126). The levels are textbook; the descriptions below are in my own words.

| Level | Owner (on the pyramid) | Question it answers | Typical decisions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corporate | Board of directors | What business are we in, and where do we put our resources? | Mission, vision and goals; which business units to hold, grow, fix or exit; capital allocation across units; the overarching strategy |
| Business | Heads of business units | How does each unit win against its competitors? | Target segment, positioning, differentiation, competitive advantage, pricing stance |
| Functional | Heads of business functions (marketing, operations, finance, HR) | What must each function do so the business strategy works? | Function goals, capabilities, budgets, policies, make-or-buy |
| Operational | Team leaders and team members | What do we do this week to deliver the plan? | Day-to-day activities and processes, resource allocation inside the team, workflow optimisation, performance management |

## The cascade-alignment check
Run it top to bottom, then bottom to top.
1. **Top-down clarity.** Can a team member state the corporate goal and their unit's way of winning in one sentence each? If not, strategy stops at a level.
2. **Each level gets a choice, not a copy.** A functional strategy that restates the business strategy has decided nothing. Ask what this function will do differently because of it, and what it will stop doing.
3. **Resources follow the levels.** Does budget and headcount allocation match the stated priorities at every level? Compare the plan with the actual spend.
4. **Bottom-up feedback.** Which operational facts (customer complaints, cost data, lost deals) reached the board last quarter, and how? If none, the cascade is one-way.
5. **Conflicts named.** Where two functions or units want opposite things (growth versus margin), who decides, and at which level?
6. **Time horizons differ.** Corporate in years, business in one to three years, functional in a year, operational in weeks. A short-horizon metric used to judge a long-horizon choice is a common failure.

## Where the pyramid misleads
- **It shows deliberate, top-down strategy only.** Mintzberg's own work stresses that realised strategy is often emergent: a pattern formed by many decisions, including ones made at the front line. The quotation on the graphic supports that reading, not the pyramid.
- **Operational is not always "strategy".** Many authors call the bottom level execution or tactics. The useful point is that it must serve the levels above.
- **Single-business firms collapse levels.** A start-up has one unit, so corporate and business strategy are the same decision. Do not invent layers.
- **Functions can become silos.** Functional strategies written separately can contradict each other. Review them together against the business strategy.
- **Ownership is a guide.** In practice the CEO and executive team own corporate and often business strategy, and the board approves and challenges it.
