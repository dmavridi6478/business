# 14 — Creative Strategy, Briefing, and Copywriting

## From Messaging Strategy to Ad Production

This document covers everything between "we know who we're targeting" and "the ad is live." It defines how to build a creative strategy, brief new concepts, write ad copy for paid social and paid search, and connect creative decisions back to performance data. It pulls from proven frameworks in the industry and integrates them with our measurement philosophy.

The creative metrics doc (`05-creative-metrics.md`) covers the diagnostic side: hook rate, hold rate, CTR, creative fatigue signals. This doc covers the strategic and production side: what to make, why, and how to write it.

---

## PART 1: THE CREATIVE STRATEGY FRAMEWORK

## The Creative Stack

Every ad is built from layers. Working top-down:

```
Pain/Desire (what problem does the product solve or what desire does it fulfill?)
    ↓
Persona (who specifically experiences this pain/desire, and in what life context?)
    ↓
Messaging Angle (the core human truth at this pain × persona intersection)
    ↓
Awareness Stage (where is this person in their journey from unaware to ready-to-buy?)
    ↓
Creative Mechanic (how does the ad make the viewer arrive at the truth?)
    ↓
Hook (the opening line or visual that stops the scroll)
    ↓
Ad Copy (AIDA framework — platform-specific structure)
    ↓
Visual Format (the production structure: video, static, carousel, UGC, etc.)
```

Most people start at the bottom (picking a format, writing a hook) without defining the layers above it. That produces random ads. This framework produces systematic creative coverage.

---

## Step 1: Determine Your Primary Anchor — Pain or Desire

Every product either solves a problem (pain) or fulfills an aspiration (desire). One of these is your primary anchor. It determines how the entire creative strategy is organized.

**Default: Use PAIN as the primary anchor.** Most ecommerce and DTC products solve functional problems. Pain-led messaging is more specific, more searchable, and more directly tied to purchase intent.

**Exception: Use DESIRE for aspirational or luxury products** where there is no acute functional problem. Fashion, luxury goods, status products, and aesthetic-driven categories are often desire-led.

**Why pain/desire is primary and not persona:** Persona alone does not create compelling messaging. "Hey busy mom, buy this water bottle" says nothing. "Hey, need a water bottle that doesn't leak all over your car while you're running errands with your kids?" says everything. The pain is what makes the message relevant. The persona adds context for how that pain is experienced.

**Pain-led examples:**
- Cystic acne, chronic fatigue, poor sleep, bulky wallet, weak WiFi, bland meal prep
- Products with clear before/after states

**Desire-led examples:**
- Timeless elegance, collector credibility, barista-level coffee at home, hotel luxury in your bathroom
- Products where the "problem" is that life could be more aspirational

---

## Step 2: Map Personas to Your Anchor

Personas are secondary. They represent different life contexts in which people experience the same pain or desire.

**The many-to-many relationship:**

One pain maps to multiple personas:
- Cystic acne → Busy Professional (perception of being unprofessional, harsh prescriptions), Stay-at-Home Mom (no time for self-care, persistent insecurity), Bride-to-be (high-stakes timeline, permanent photos)
- Each experiences the SAME pain but in different life contexts, which means different messaging

One persona can experience multiple pains:
- Busy Professional → Cystic acne, chronic fatigue, poor sleep quality
- Different pains, different messaging angles

**This creates a matrix:**

```
                    Busy Pro    Stay-Home Mom    Bride
Cystic Acne            ✓             ✓            ✓
Folliculitis           ✓             ✓           
Boils                                ✓            ✓
```

Each ✓ is a unique messaging angle opportunity. This is the creative strategy landscape.

**Define each persona by:**
- Demographics: age range, gender, role, life stage
- Psychographics: values, challenges, daily context
- Most importantly: how they experience this specific pain/desire differently from other personas

---

## Step 3: Define Messaging Angles

A messaging angle is the core truth for a specific pain × persona intersection. It is not a tagline or slogan. It is a conversational, human statement that captures the insight.

**Examples:**

| Pain/Desire | Persona | Messaging Angle |
|---|---|---|
| Bulky Wallet | Minimalist professional | "Your wallet sucks" |
| Bored Cat (destructive) | Cat owner with shredded furniture | "Your cat isn't broken, they're bored" |
| Poor Sleep | Exhausted professional | "Melatonin stopped working three months ago" |
| Cystic Acne | Professional burned by dermatologist | "Your dermatologist wrecked your skin" |

**Notice:** Same pain, different persona = different angle. That is the power of the intersection.

**For each intersection, define:**
1. **Use case:** How exactly does THIS person experience THIS pain?
2. **Deepest desire:** What do they really want (beyond the surface)?
3. **Feature/benefit priorities:** Which product aspects matter most to THEM specifically?
4. **Top objections:** What would make THEM specifically hesitate?

**The messaging angle statement should be:**
- Conversational, not corporate ("Your dermatologist wrecked your skin" not "Professional-grade natural healing")
- Something a real person would say or think
- Specific to the intersection, not generic to the product

---

## Step 4: Map Across the 5 Awareness Stages

Every messaging angle deploys across all five stages. The angle stays the same. What changes is how you express it based on where the person is in their journey.

**Stage 1: Unaware** — Does not know they have a problem
- Goal: Make them aware the problem exists
- Approach: Education, revelation, relatable scenarios
- Do not mention the product

**Stage 2: Problem-Aware** — Knows the problem, has not found a solution
- Goal: Agitate the pain, validate their experience
- Approach: Empathy, pain callouts, "tired of...?" framing

**Stage 3: Solution-Aware** — Knows solutions exist, exploring options
- Goal: Position your solution, differentiate from alternatives
- Approach: Category education, comparison, "there's a better way"

**Stage 4: Product-Aware** — Knows your product, considering it vs. alternatives
- Goal: Overcome objections, prove superiority
- Approach: Proof, comparisons, guarantees, reviews

**Stage 5: Most-Aware** — Ready to purchase, needs a final push
- Goal: Close the sale
- Approach: Urgency, guarantees, direct CTA, limited offers

**Example: "Your dermatologist wrecked your skin" across all 5 stages:**
- Unaware: "If your skin is worse after seeing the dermatologist..."
- Problem-Aware: "Why does your prescription burn more than your acne?"
- Solution-Aware: "Natural treatments that heal without destroying your skin barrier"
- Product-Aware: "[Brand] vs your dermatologist's prescription: 847 five-star reviews"
- Most-Aware: "Get [Brand] with our 30-day guarantee. If it doesn't work, keep it free."

**Budget determines stage priority:**
- Lower budgets ($3K-$10K/month): Start with Problem-Aware and Solution-Aware. Find people already looking.
- Scaling budgets ($10K+/month): Invest in Unaware content. Grow the top of the funnel.
- The key to scaling is locking in diverse early-stage messaging across multiple pain × persona intersections.

## Step 5: Choose the Creative Mechanic

Before writing the hook, define the creative mechanic. This is the structural mechanism by which the ad makes the viewer arrive at the truth. The mechanic is not what you say (hook) or what it looks like (format). It is the cognitive or emotional move the ad makes.

### The Mechanic Library

**Implied Answer**
The hook poses a question. The visuals silently answer it. No one states the answer. The viewer concludes it themselves.

Why it works: Self-conclusions bypass ad skepticism. The insight lands as the viewer's own thought, not a brand claim.

Example: Hook: "Why does she always stay home?" Visual: Woman in an incredible living room with beautiful decor. Implied truth: Her home is so good she does not need to leave. Brand: Home decor.

Best for: Unaware, Problem-Aware

---

**Social Witness**
Someone other than the customer notices the change. A compliment, a double-take, an unsolicited "what are you doing differently?" The product's impact is validated through a third party.

Why it works: Third-party validation is more believable than self-reported results. It implies the change is visible enough that other people notice.

Example: Woman at work, colleague stops her: "Wait, did you do something different? Your hair looks incredible." She just smiles. Product shown at end, no voiceover.

Best for: Problem-Aware, Product-Aware

---

**Overheard Conversation**
The ad is framed as something you were not supposed to see: a text thread, a DM, a group chat. It feels like eavesdropping, not advertising.

Why it works: Removes the "this is an ad" filter entirely. Viewers process it as social content. Informal framing makes claims more credible.

Example: iMessage thread. Friend 1: "okay don't judge me but I've been using this probiotic." Friend 2: "omg which one." Friend 1 screenshots the product. Ad ends.

Best for: Unaware, Problem-Aware

---

**The Reframe**
Opens by validating a belief the viewer already holds. Then flips the frame entirely. The product is not the hero. The new perspective is.

Why it works: People share things that changed how they think. The Reframe manufactures the "I never thought about it that way" moment, which drives both conversion and organic sharing.

Example: "You've been told you just need 8 hours of sleep." Shows person doing everything right. Flip: "The problem was never the hours. It was the quality." Product: the thing that explains why they are still exhausted.

Best for: Problem-Aware, Solution-Aware

---

**Borrowed Enemy**
Describes a problem obviously caused by a specific competitor without ever naming them. The viewer makes the connection.

Why it works: Naming a competitor triggers defensiveness and legal risk. Not naming them but making the reference unmistakable lets the viewer do the conquesting themselves.

Example: "That jittery crash feeling... ingredients you can't pronounce... wondering what you just put in your body." Every Red Bull drinker knows exactly who this is about. Product presented as the obvious alternative.

Best for: Solution-Aware, Product-Aware

---

**Trojan Horse**
The ad looks like educational content, entertainment, or a personal story until the final 20%, where the product appears naturally. The viewer is emotionally invested before they realize it is an ad.

Why it works: Ad avoidance is highest at the moment of recognition. The Trojan Horse delays recognition until the viewer is already engaged.

Example: Opens as a genuine cooking tutorial on how to properly dice an onion. Full technique breakdown, genuinely useful. Two minutes in: "and obviously it helps that this knife actually holds an edge." Product was barely the point.

Best for: Unaware audiences who would scroll past a traditional ad

---

**Contrast Without Comment**
Shows two realities side by side (before/after, with/without) but never editorially tells the viewer which is better. The visual makes it obvious.

Why it works: Stating the obvious feels like an ad. Showing it and letting the viewer conclude feels like evidence. The absence of editorializing is a credibility signal.

Example: Split screen, no voiceover. Left: groggy wake-up, hitting snooze. Right: waking up naturally before the alarm, stretching, looking rested. Nothing said. Everything communicated.

Best for: Problem-Aware, Solution-Aware

---

### Mechanic Selection Guide

| If you want the viewer to... | Use this mechanic |
|---|---|
| Conclude the truth themselves from a visual | Implied Answer |
| Believe results through someone else's reaction | Social Witness |
| Lower their ad guard completely | Overheard Conversation |
| Have a "I never thought about it that way" moment | Reframe |
| Recognize a competitor without you naming them | Borrowed Enemy |
| Engage before they realize it is an ad | Trojan Horse |
| Feel the gap without being told what to think | Contrast Without Comment |

**Mechanics can be layered.** A primary mechanic shapes the concept architecture. A secondary adds depth. Common pairings: Implied Answer + Social Witness, Trojan Horse + Reframe, Overheard Conversation + Social Witness.

## Step 6: Write the Hook

The hook is the opening 1-3 seconds that stops the scroll. It is a tactical expression of the messaging angle at a specific awareness stage, using a specific psychological trigger.

### The 8 Psychological Triggers

Every effective hook leverages at least one. The best combine two.

**1. Pattern Interrupt** — Break mental autopilot. Say something unexpected or counterintuitive.
"Stop moisturizing your face."

**2. Identity Call-Out** — Make the right people self-select immediately.
"If you're a bride with cystic acne, watch this."

**3. Pain Agitation** — Mirror their internal monologue. Make the pain feel acute.
"You've tried everything and your skin is still breaking out."

**4. Curiosity Gap** — Create an open loop they need to close.
"Nobody's talking about this Meta update."

**5. Social Proof / Credibility** — Real people, real results, real specificity.
"I've been breaking out since I was 12. Here's what finally worked at 29."

**6. Contrarian / Myth-Busting** — Challenge a belief they hold.
"Retinol is the reason your skin keeps purging."

**7. Aspiration / Desire** — Show them what is possible on the other side.
"You're 6 weeks away from not thinking about your skin anymore."

**8. Urgency / Stakes** — Make inaction feel costly.
"Your wedding is in 8 weeks. What are you doing about your skin?"

### Hook Tactics Reference (35 Named Tactics)

Beyond the 8 psychological triggers, there are specific tactical frames for hooks. Tactics are the frame. Triggers are the mechanism inside the frame. A few high-value tactics worth knowing:

| Tactic | What it is | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Contrarian | Goes against conventional wisdom | Educated, skeptical audiences |
| Confession | Candid admission that builds credibility through vulnerability | Rebuilding trust, countering skepticism |
| Contrast | Juxtaposes two things to highlight a mismatch | Price-sensitive, competitive conquesting |
| Borrowed Enemy | Describes a competitor's problem without naming them | Solution-Aware, Product-Aware |
| Reframe | Opens by validating then flipping a belief | Problem-Aware who have tried and failed |
| Social Proof | Leverages reviews, testimonials, or popularity | Product-Aware with hesitation |
| Warning | Issues a sincere caution | Problem-Aware about to make a mistake |
| Price Anchor | Frames cost against a familiar benchmark | Mid-to-high ticket, price-sensitive |
| Reverse Psychology | Tells the viewer NOT to act to trigger reactance | Ad-fatigued, contrarian personas |
| Demographic Callout | Names a specific audience to force self-selection | Niche products, segmented audiences |
| Storytelling | Drops mid-moment into a personal story | Any stage, especially emotional products |

**Hook writing standards:**
- Write in the reader's voice, not the brand's voice
- Lead with the pain/desire, not the product
- Use specific numbers, timeframes, and details
- Read it out loud. If it sounds like an ad, rewrite it.
- Vary the psychological trigger across your hook set. Do not write 5 hooks that all use the same trigger.

## PART 2: THE AIDA COPYWRITING FRAMEWORK

Once the creative strategy is defined (pain, persona, messaging angle, awareness stage, mechanic, hook), the next step is writing the actual ad copy. We use the AIDA framework (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action) but it applies differently depending on the platform.

---

## Paid Social Copy (Meta, TikTok, etc.)

Paid social ads have four distinct text fields, and each maps to a specific AIDA element:

### The Structure

**1. In-Ad Creative Text (Big Headline on the creative itself) → ATTENTION**
- Bold, short, eye-catching
- Think of it like a billboard headline
- Maximum 7 words
- This is the visual hook that stops the scroll alongside the image or video

**2. Primary Text (above the ad in the feed) → INTEREST**
- Can be longer (2-4 sentences typical)
- The first sentence must also serve as an attention grabber (because some users read this first)
- Speak directly to the user with "you" language
- Expand on the value, benefit, or story
- This is where the messaging angle comes through most clearly

**3. Headline (below the ad creative) → DESIRE**
- Short, powerful, outcome-focused
- Begin with a verb: Get, Stop, Begin, Discover, Feel, Change, etc.
- Describe "what could be" if the user clicks (pleasure vs. pain framing)
- Examples: "Get Faster Shipping", "Stop Guessing What Works", "Feel the Difference in 7 Days"

**4. Description Text (optional supporting copy below headline) → SUPPORTING**
- Simple supporting statement
- Highlight a feature ("Made with organic ingredients") or a benefit ("Free shipping over $45")
- Does not need to follow AIDA. Just adds context.

**5. Call to Action Button → ACTION**
- Choose from Meta's preset CTA options (Shop Now, Learn More, Get Offer, Sign Up)
- Must match the funnel stage:
  - Awareness campaigns = Learn More
  - Consideration campaigns = Learn More or Sign Up
  - Conversion campaigns = Shop Now or Get Offer

### Paid Social Copy Rules

- Direct, persuasive, conversion-focused tone
- No fluff, no filler
- Short, punchy sentences
- Always produce 3 variations unless instructed otherwise
- Never use em dashes
- Never use: Boost, Elevate, Revolutionize, Streamline, Unlock, or any word that sounds like a SaaS landing page wrote it

### Example Output Format

```
VARIATION 1

Primary Text (Interest):
Tired of spending $5K/month on ads and having no idea what's actually working? 
You're not bad at ads. You're just measuring wrong. Most brands rely on 
platform ROAS and wonder why the CFO doesn't trust the numbers.

Big Creative Headline (Attention):
Your ROAS Is Lying to You

Headline Below Ad (Desire):
Stop Guessing. Start Measuring What Matters.

Description (Supporting Text):
Incrementality testing for ecommerce brands.

CTA: Learn More
```

---

## Paid Search Copy (Google Ads RSAs)

Paid search copy follows AIDA but in a fundamentally different way than social. The searcher has declared intent through their query. Your job is to match that intent, not interrupt a scroll.

### The Structure

**Headline 1 (H1) → ATTENTION**
- Always match the searcher's intent
- Ideally use the exact phrase from the search query or a close match
- Title Case
- 28 characters max (not 30, stay safe for truncation)

**Headline 2 (H2) → INTEREST**
- Add a feature, benefit, capability, promo, or social proof that strengthens H1
- Title Case
- 28 characters max

**Headline 3 (H3, optional) → ACTION**
- Rarely shown by Google, but if used, keep it as a short CTA
- "Buy Today", "Try It Free", "Shop the Collection"

**Description → DESIRE + ACTION**
- 85 characters max (not 90, stay safe)
- Must start with a verb
- Describe "what could be" if the user clicks (the positive outcome or pain avoided)
- End with a unique 3-4 word CTA so the ad always completes the AIDA cycle

### Paid Search Copy Generation

For every keyword or ad group, generate:

**Headlines:**
- 5 Attention options (H1, match intent)
- 5 Feature options (H2)
- 5 Benefit options (H2)
- 5 Capability options (H2)
- 5 Social Proof options (H2)
- 3 CTA options (H3)

**Descriptions:**
- At least 5 options
- Each starts with a verb
- Each ends with a unique 3-4 word CTA (different in each variation)

### Example Output Format

For keyword: "best running shoes for women"

```
ATTENTION HEADLINES (H1 — match intent):
1. Women's Running Shoes
2. Best Running Shoes for Her
3. Top-Rated Women's Runners
4. Running Shoes Built for Women
5. Women's Performance Running

INTEREST HEADLINES (H2 — features):
1. Lightweight & Breathable Design
2. Advanced Cushion Technology
3. Available in Wide Widths
4. Zero Break-In Period
5. Rated Best by Runners World

INTEREST HEADLINES (H2 — benefits):
1. Run Further Without Pain
2. Feel the Difference Instantly
3. Your Best Run Is Next
4. Comfort That Lasts for Miles
5. No More Blisters or Hot Spots

INTEREST HEADLINES (H2 — social proof):
1. 12,000+ Five-Star Reviews
2. Worn by Olympic Athletes
3. #1 Rated by Real Runners
4. Trusted by 50,000 Women
5. Award-Winning Comfort

CTA HEADLINES (H3):
1. Shop the Collection
2. Find Your Perfect Fit
3. Order Today

DESCRIPTIONS (Desire + Action):
1. Run further and recover faster with shoes designed for a woman's stride. Find your pair now.
2. Feel the difference from your first step with cushioning that adapts to your gait. Shop the collection.
3. Join 50,000 women who stopped settling for shoes that weren't made for them. Try yours today.
4. Experience zero break-in comfort and lasting support mile after mile. Get fitted online.
5. Discover the shoe that runners are calling a game changer for long-distance comfort. Order now.
```

### Key Differences: Paid Social vs. Paid Search Copy

| Dimension | Paid Social | Paid Search |
|---|---|---|
| User state | Interrupting a scroll (no intent) | Responding to declared intent |
| ATTENTION job | Stop the scroll, pattern interrupt | Match the search query exactly |
| Primary text role | Build interest, expand the story | N/A (no equivalent field) |
| Headline role | Create desire (outcome-focused) | Create interest (features/proof) |
| Tone | Conversational, native to feed | Concise, keyword-rich, direct |
| Character limits | Flexible (primary text can be long) | Strict (28 char headlines, 85 char descriptions) |
| CTA matching | Funnel stage determines CTA | Always action-oriented |

## PART 3: CONNECTING CREATIVE TO MEASUREMENT

This is the section Alysha's repo does not have and it is the most important part. Creative strategy without a measurement feedback loop is flying blind.

### The Creative Feedback Loop

```
Creative Strategy (this doc)
    ↓ produces
Ads in the account
    ↓ generates
Performance data (05-creative-metrics.md)
    ↓ analyzed during
Weekly optimization (12-weekly-optimization-cadence.md)
    ↓ validated by
Three pillars of measurement (13-three-pillars-of-measurement.md)
    ↓ feeds back into
Creative Strategy (this doc)
```

### How Each Measurement Pillar Informs Creative

**Pillar 1: MTA (platform data) → Operational creative decisions**
- Hook rate tells you if the opening is working (see `05-creative-metrics.md`)
- Hold rate tells you if the body content is engaging
- CTR tells you if the CTA is compelling
- CVR tells you if the message-to-landing-page handoff is coherent
- Creative fatigue signals tell you when to refresh
- MTA is useful for comparing ad A vs. ad B within a platform

**Pillar 2: Post-purchase surveys → Strategic creative decisions**
- "How did you first hear about us?" reveals which channels and messaging angles are actually resonating (often different from what MTA shows)
- "What convinced you to buy today?" reveals which messaging angles are driving the final purchase decision. If customers keep saying "I saw the video about X," that is your winning angle.
- "What almost stopped you from purchasing?" reveals which objections your creative needs to address. This feeds directly into Product-Aware and Most-Aware hooks.
- Store survey results in `clients/[name]/post-purchase-survey-results/`

**Pillar 3: Causal measurement → Channel and budget allocation**
- Incrementality tests reveal whether a creative approach (or channel) is actually driving conversions that would not have happened otherwise
- This is especially critical for upper-funnel creative (Unaware, Problem-Aware stages) that MTA systematically under-credits
- If a Trojan Horse or Overheard Conversation mechanic has low platform ROAS but causal testing shows high incrementality, that is a winning creative you should scale, not cut

### What to Track by Creative Element

| Element | Metric to Watch | Where to Find It |
|---|---|---|
| Hook (first 3 seconds) | Hook rate, thumb stop rate | Platform metrics (Pillar 1) |
| Body content | Hold rate, retention curve | Platform metrics (Pillar 1) |
| CTA | Outbound CTR | Platform metrics (Pillar 1) |
| Messaging angle | Survey responses ("what convinced you?") | Post-purchase surveys (Pillar 2) |
| Overall creative approach | iROAS, incremental CM | Causal measurement (Pillar 3) |
| Creative fatigue | Hook rate decline, CTR decline, frequency rise | Platform metrics (Pillar 1) |

### Using Data to Prioritize the Creative Matrix

The creative strategy framework above can produce dozens or hundreds of possible messaging angles (every pain × persona × awareness stage × mechanic). You cannot make all of them. Data tells you which to prioritize:

**If you have survey data:** Prioritize messaging angles that align with what customers say convinced them. If 30% of survey respondents mention a specific pain point, that pain bucket gets more creative investment.

**If you have platform data:** Look at hook rate and hold rate by messaging angle. If "Your dermatologist wrecked your skin" hooks consistently get 35%+ hook rates while "Natural healing for sensitive skin" hooks get 18%, invest more creative volume in the winning angle.

**If you have causal data:** Allocate creative production budget toward the channels and awareness stages that are proven incremental. If a geo-lift test shows YouTube awareness creative is driving $3 incremental revenue per $1 spent, invest in more Unaware and Problem-Aware creative for YouTube even if MTA says it is underperforming.

**If you have nothing yet:** Start with 3-5 messaging angles covering different pains and personas. Run them for 2-4 weeks. Let the data tell you where to go deeper.

## PART 4: REVIEW MINING — TURNING CUSTOMER LANGUAGE INTO AD COPY

Before writing any creative, mine customer reviews. Customers describe their pain, their trigger moment, their objections, and their transformation in language no copywriter can invent. This is the qualitative input that makes ad copy feel real.

### The 5 Buckets

**Bucket 1: Pain Points** — What problem were they experiencing before the product?
Look for: descriptions of the problem, how long they had it, what they tried before, how it affected their life, the emotional weight of living with it.

**Bucket 2: Trigger Moments** — What finally made them buy?
Look for: the specific moment, event, or realization that turned a maybe into a purchase. A life event, a recommendation, hitting a breaking point, running out of patience with other solutions.

**Bucket 3: Objections Before Purchasing** — What almost stopped them?
Look for: skepticism mentioned in past tense ("I was skeptical but..."), comparisons to products that failed, price hesitation, disbelief that it would work. These are gold for Product-Aware and Most-Aware ad copy.

**Bucket 4: Transformations** — What changed after using the product?
Look for: specific results, how their life is different, the emotional shift (confidence, relief, freedom), and how they describe the change in their own words.

**Bucket 5: Standout Language** — Exact phrases worth stealing
The most vivid, emotionally charged, specific lines from across all buckets. Lines that could be a hook with zero editing. Phrases that capture an emotion in a way you could not have written yourself.

### How Review Data Feeds the Creative Stack

- Pain Points → Define pain buckets for Step 1
- Trigger Moments → Powerful hook material (the exact moment of emotional readiness)
- Objections → Feed Product-Aware hooks, Borrowed Enemy mechanic, Risk Reversal tactics
- Transformations → Feed Social Proof hooks, Social Witness mechanic, Aspiration triggers
- Standout Language → Direct input for ad copy that mirrors how customers actually talk

## PART 5: PUTTING IT ALL TOGETHER

### The Creative Briefing Workflow

For each client, the full workflow is:

1. **Define the anchor** (pain or desire) and map personas to it
2. **Mine reviews** (if available) to extract the 5 buckets of customer language
3. **Define 3-5 messaging angles** at the highest-priority pain × persona intersections
4. **Choose awareness stages** to prioritize based on budget and funnel health
5. **Select creative mechanics** for each concept
6. **Write hooks** using the psychological triggers, varying triggers across the set
7. **Write ad copy** using the AIDA framework (social or search, platform-specific)
8. **Choose visual formats** and produce the creative
9. **Launch, measure, and iterate** — weekly optimization feeds back into the next round

### Priority Order for Smaller Clients ($3-5M revenue)

Not every client needs 50 messaging angles and full-funnel coverage. For smaller brands:

1. Start with 2-3 pain buckets with 1-2 personas each = 4-6 messaging angles
2. Focus on Problem-Aware and Solution-Aware stages first (most efficient at lower budgets)
3. Use 2-3 creative mechanics max (Reframe and Social Witness are the most versatile)
4. Write 3-5 hooks per angle, varying psychological triggers
5. Run for 2-4 weeks, review performance data, then expand the angles and stages that are working

### The Strategic Gap Analysis

Once you have creative running, audit the matrix quarterly:

- Which pain × persona intersections have not been tested?
- Which awareness stages are under-represented? (Most brands over-index on Product-Aware and Most-Aware)
- Which creative mechanics have not been tried?
- Are there new personas emerging from survey data?
- Is the creative winning on MTA also winning on causal measurement?
