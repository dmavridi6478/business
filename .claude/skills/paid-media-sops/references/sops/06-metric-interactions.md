# 06 — Metric Interaction Effects

## When Metrics Move in Opposite Directions

Optimizing one metric in isolation can degrade another. This document covers the most common interaction effects and how to evaluate tradeoffs.

---

## Core Principle: Metrics Are Not Independent

The five core metrics are connected through the revenue chain. Pulling one lever often moves another — sometimes in the opposite direction. A media buyer who chases a single metric (lowest CPM, highest CTR) without understanding the downstream effects will make the account worse.

**The job is not to maximize any single metric. The job is to optimize the *system* — the combination of all five metrics that produces the best business outcome.**

---

## Common Interaction Effects

### 1. Broadening Targeting: CPM ↓ but CTR ↓

**What happens:** When you expand from narrow audiences (lookalikes, interests) to broad targeting, CPMs often decrease because there is less auction competition for broad inventory. However, CTR also drops because you are now showing ads to people who are less pre-qualified.

**How to evaluate:**

Calculate the *net impact on CPC*:
- Before: CPM $20, CTR 2.0% → CPC = $20 / (0.02 × 1,000) = $1.00
- After: CPM $14, CTR 1.2% → CPC = $14 / (0.012 × 1,000) = $1.17

In this case, the CPM savings did not fully offset the CTR decline — CPC actually rose 17%. But this is not the full picture.

Now check CVR:
- Before CVR: 2.0% → CPA = $1.00 / 0.02 = $50
- After CVR: 1.8% → CPA = $1.17 / 0.018 = $65

CPA rose 30%. The broadening did not pay off in this scenario.

**But consider this alternative:**
- After: CPM $12, CTR 1.4%, CVR 1.6% → CPA = ($12 / (0.014 × 1,000)) / 0.016 = $53.57

Here the broadening raised CPA only 7% while significantly increasing the addressable audience. At scale, this might generate more total conversions at an acceptable marginal CPA.

**Rule:** When broadening targeting, evaluate CPA and total conversion volume together. A modest CPA increase with a large volume increase is often the right trade.

---

### 2. New Creative: CTR ↑ but CVR ↓

**What happens:** New creative can attract more clicks from a broader set of people (curiosity clicks, entertainment-driven clicks) who are less likely to convert. The creative is effective at earning attention but not at qualifying intent.

**How to evaluate:**

| Scenario | CTR | CVR | CPA | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Old creative | 1.5% | 2.2% | $38.38 | Baseline |
| New creative A | 2.8% | 1.0% | $38.10 | Wash — more clicks but fewer convert. CPA is similar but the funnel is less efficient. |
| New creative B | 2.4% | 1.6% | $27.90 | Win — CTR up enough and CVR held well enough that CPA improved. |
| New creative C | 3.5% | 0.6% | $40.48 | Loss — CTR skyrocketed but CVR collapsed. The creative is attracting the wrong clicks. |

**Rule:** CTR is not a performance metric by itself. CTR × CVR × AOV is. A high-CTR ad that tanks CVR is not a good ad — it is an expensive distraction.

---

### 3. Scaling Spend: All Metrics Degrade (The Scale Tax)

**What happens:** Increasing budget forces the algorithm to find additional impressions beyond the most efficient inventory. Each marginal dollar buys slightly worse impressions. This manifests as:
- CPM ↑ (more expensive inventory needed)
- CTR ↓ (broader, less-interested audiences)
- CVR ↓ (lower-intent traffic)

**How to evaluate:**

Calculate the **marginal CPA** — the CPA of only the *incremental* conversions generated by the additional spend.

**Example:**

| | Spend | Conversions | CPA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-scale | $5,000 | 100 | $50 |
| Post-scale | $7,500 | 130 | $57.69 |
| Incremental | $2,500 | 30 | $83.33 |

The blended CPA rose from $50 to $57.69 (15% increase), which may seem acceptable. But the marginal CPA of the incremental 30 conversions is $83.33 — 67% higher than baseline. The question is whether $83.33 per incremental conversion is profitable for the business.

**Rule:** Always calculate marginal CPA when scaling. Blended CPA masks the true cost of growth. Scale in 15-20% increments and re-evaluate after 5-7 days at each new level.

---

### 4. Improving LP (CVR ↑ but AOV ↓)

**What happens:** Landing page optimizations that reduce friction (simpler checkout, more prominent discount, lower-priced product featured) can boost CVR while pulling down AOV. More people convert, but they buy less.

**How to evaluate:**

Calculate **revenue per click (RPC)**:

| Scenario | CVR | AOV | RPC (CVR × AOV) | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before LP change | 2.0% | $80 | $1.60 | Baseline |
| After LP change A | 3.2% | $55 | $1.76 | Win — more revenue per click despite lower AOV |
| After LP change B | 2.8% | $52 | $1.46 | Loss — CVR increase did not offset AOV decline |

**Rule:** Revenue per click (RPC) or revenue per session is a better north star than CVR or AOV in isolation. The LP that generates the most revenue per visitor wins, regardless of which metric moved.

---

### 5. Frequency Increase: CPM Stable but CTR ↓ and CVR ↓

**What happens:** As frequency rises, the same people see the same ads repeatedly. They stop clicking (CTR ↓) and those who do click are increasingly unlikely to convert (CVR ↓). CPM may stay flat because the cost per impression has not changed — you are just buying more impressions from the same people.

**This is invisible if you only watch CPM and spend.** Everything looks normal at the top of the funnel while performance degrades at the bottom.

**How to detect:**

| Check | Signal |
|---|---|
| CPMr rising while CPM is flat | Frequency is increasing — cost per unique person reached is going up |
| Frequency metric > 3.0 in a 7-day window | Most audiences experience fatigue above 3 exposures per week |
| CTR declining while impressions are stable | Same people, same ads, fewer clicks |
| Reach flattening while spend is constant | The algorithm has saturated the audience |

**Rule:** Monitor frequency and CPMr alongside CPM. A "stable CPM" can hide an account that is burning out its audience.

---

### 6. Discount/Promotion: CVR ↑, AOV ↓, Volume ↑

**What happens:** Running a promotion (20% off, BOGO, free shipping) typically lifts CVR (lower friction to purchase) while pulling down AOV (lower revenue per order). The net impact depends on the margin structure.

**How to evaluate:**

| | CVR | AOV | Orders (per 1K clicks) | Revenue (per 1K clicks) | CPA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No promo | 2.0% | $80 | 20 | $1,600 | $50 |
| 20% off | 3.5% | $64 | 35 | $2,240 | $28.57 |
| Free ship ($8 cost) | 2.8% | $78 | 28 | $2,184 | $35.71 |
| BOGO 50% off | 3.0% | $90 | 30 | $2,700 | $33.33 |

**Critical check:** Revenue is not profit. Calculate the *margin impact*:

| | Revenue/1K clicks | Discount cost | Margin per order (est.) | Total margin/1K clicks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No promo | $1,600 | $0 | $32 | $640 |
| 20% off | $2,240 | $16/order | $12.80 | $448 |
| Free ship | $2,184 | $8/order | $23.20 | $649.60 |
| BOGO 50% | $2,700 | $22.50/order | $22.50 | $675 |

In this example, the 20% off promotion actually *destroyed* margin despite looking great on CVR and CPA. Free shipping and BOGO performed better on a margin basis.

**Rule:** Evaluate promotions on margin, not revenue. A promotion that lifts CVR but destroys margin is not a win — it is buying conversions at a loss.

## The Interaction Effects Summary Table

| Change Made | Likely Positive Effect | Likely Negative Effect | Net Evaluation Metric |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broaden targeting | CPM ↓, Scale ↑ | CTR ↓, CVR ↓ | CPA + total conversions |
| Narrow targeting | CTR ↑, CVR ↑ | CPM ↑, Scale ↓ | CPA + total conversions |
| New creative (broader appeal) | CTR ↑ | CVR ↓ | RPC (revenue per click) |
| New creative (more qualifying) | CVR ↑ | CTR ↓ | RPC (revenue per click) |
| Increase budget | Volume ↑ | CPM ↑, CTR ↓, CVR ↓ | Marginal CPA |
| Decrease budget | CPM ↓, CPA ↓ | Volume ↓ | Total conversions at target CPA |
| Run promotion | CVR ↑ | AOV ↓ | Margin per click |
| Raise price | AOV ↑ | CVR ↓ | Revenue per click + margin |
| Add retargeting | CVR ↑ (blended) | May cannibalize organic | Incremental lift (holdout test) |
| New placement | CPM change (up or down) | CTR/CVR uncertainty | CPA by placement |

## The Optimization Paradox

The most dangerous trap in paid media is optimizing a single metric without understanding the system-level effect. Common examples:

- **"Get CPMs as low as possible"** → Leads to low-quality placements (Audience Network, right column) that destroy CTR and CVR. Net CPA goes up.
- **"Maximize CTR"** → Leads to clickbait creative that attracts curiosity clicks without purchase intent. CVR craters. Net CPA goes up.
- **"Maximize CVR"** → Leads to spending only on retargeting (where CVR is naturally high) while starving prospecting. Short-term CPA looks great but new customer acquisition dies.
- **"Maximize AOV"** → Leads to targeting only high-income audiences or promoting only premium products. Volume drops. Total revenue may decline even though per-order revenue is up.

**The correct approach:** Define the business-level objective (target CPA, target ROAS, target contribution margin, or total revenue goal), then optimize the system of five metrics to achieve it. No single metric is the goal — the business outcome is the goal.
