# Nine go-to-market models

Source: one infographic ("Nail your product-market fit"). What each diagram shows is read from the image; "Use it when" and the order of use are my reading and are marked as such. Credit: Marceline Tsobgny, NipPro AI.

| # | Model | What the infographic shows | Question it answers | Use it when (my reading) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bullseye Framework | Three rings around a centre: outer rings hold channel ideas (Website and SEO, PR and ads, Social media, Email, Community, Sales support, Events, Direct marketing, Partnerships, Content, Product marketing, Research). Caption: outer brainstorm, middle test, inner focus | Which channel should get the effort? | You have many channel ideas and little time |
| 2 | Positioning to Messaging | A house: a positioning statement on top, then USPs, problem and solution, key messages per customer profile and per journey stage, and messaging guidelines split into promotional copy (website, ads, flyers, catalogues, videos, pitch decks) and core copy (messages in the product, user instructions, customer support) | What do we say, and where? | Copy is inconsistent across channels |
| 3 | Growth Loops | Five loops with example companies' logos: viral, referral, usage-based, user-generated and collaboration | How does growth feed itself? | Paid or sales-led growth costs too much |
| 4 | PMF Cycle | Start with a big vision and a product hypothesis, then identify a beachhead segment, create a minimum viable business, build a minimum viable product, test, analyse, learn, and ask "Did it work?"; yes ends at "PMF proven" | Do we have product-market fit? | You need a loop for testing, not a one-off launch |
| 5 | Minimum Viable Segment | Three overlapping circles: willingness to pay (WTP), minimum viable product (MVP) and minimum viable segment (MVS); PMF sits where all three meet. Note: "Your sweet spot: smaller segment, easier to cover" | Who is the smallest group we can win? | Your market feels too broad |
| 6 | Van Westendorp's Price Meter | Four curves (too cheap, not a bargain, not expensive, too expensive) with PMC, OPP and PME marked and a shaded "range of acceptable prices"; caption "4 questions, your acceptable price range" | What price range will buyers accept? | You must price before you have usage data |
| 7 | Beachhead Strategy | "Spray and pray" with effort spread thin, against three waves: win the beachhead segment first, expand to adjacent markets, then conquer other segments | Where do we start, and in what order do we expand? | You are tempted to chase every segment at once |
| 8 | ECP vs ICP | Early customer profile: an early adopter who tries new tech, easy to work with and self-onboarding, generous with feedback, a great beta tester, will gladly endorse you. Ideal customer profile: would make a great logo, hard to close, "go big or go home", requires proof of work, takes time to onboard, once in they are in it to win it | Who do we sell to first, and who later? | Early deals stall because you chase large accounts too soon |
| 9 | Validation Methods | A chart of confidence against product completeness, from "a signal" to "MVP done": interviews, surveys, solution testing, marketing tests, waitlists, co-creation with a client, presale, testing an MVP | How much proof can we get before building? | You are about to build on an assumption |

## A sequence that fits the nine (my reading, not on the slide)

1. **Who:** minimum viable segment (5), beachhead (7), ECP before ICP (8).
2. **Proof:** validation methods (9), then the PMF cycle (4).
3. **Message and price:** positioning to messaging (2), price meter (6).
4. **Growth:** bullseye (1), then growth loops (3).

## Van Westendorp: read this before using the chart

The four questions are about too cheap, cheap, expensive and too expensive. Sources define the marked points slightly differently. The slide labels its curves "not a bargain" and "not expensive", the inverse-curve convention; other sources define PMC as where "too cheap" crosses "expensive" and PME as where "too expensive" crosses "cheap". Search results I read summarise OPP as where "too cheap" and "too expensive" cross, and IPP as the price where equal numbers call it cheap and expensive; I did not open the pages. The acceptable range between PMC and PME is the main output; OPP is often over-read and says nothing about volume or margin. State which convention you used. See `pricing` (its references already cover this method).

## Not on the slide

No sample sizes, no sources and no evidence that any model works better than another. Treat each diagram as a prompt for a conversation, not a result.
